INVESTIGACIÓN

I study legal technique – the set of practices through which law is produced, including legal provisions, organizational procedures and ordinary objects – from a sociocultural perspective. In my work, I argue that what may appear merely instrumental from a legal perspective can shape broader aspects of society. My research is organized around four main themes:

Legal technique and crime

I am interested in what the use of legal provisions, procedures and objects can tell us about society more broadly. In my PhD research, I drew on my fieldwork in Chile to show how the legal notion of arrest in flagrante delicto structures perceptions of crime and configures a specific truth-making regime. I have developed this argument in articles published in the Law & Society Review and in Droit et société. Believing that researchers should make their work accessible to the people and communities we write about, I am turning my PhD research into a book in Spanish, titled Governing through Flagrant Crime.

Beyond flagrante delicto, I have explored a more familiar technology of Chile’s criminal justice system: crime statistics. I have written about crime statistics in general and car theft statistics in particular. No matter how accurate these statistics are, people don’t seem to believe them.

Legal technique and jurisdiction

Jurisdiction is the authority to apply the law within a specific territory or field. In my work, I am interested in documenting how, in practice and despite what the law “in the books” provides, jurisdiction can be effectively exercised or constrained. For example, colleagues at the Institut québécois de réforme du droit et de la justice (IQRDJ) and I explored how states of emergency defined by different statutes in Quebec broadened or restricted the possibilities for exercising public authority. We showed how, during the state of emergency declared for the COVID-19 pandemic, public officials acquired new de facto prerogatives. Surprisingly, we also found that the opposite occurred with local states of emergency declared by municipalities in response to disasters such as floods and wildfires: municipalities sometimes found themselves with less rather than more power.

Legal technique and dematerialization

Justice systems rely on specific material arrangements, including files, courtroom layouts and recording devices. Although seemingly minor compared with principles of fundamental justice, such material arrangements are integral to the functioning of justice systems. Employing an indexical approach to documents (as described in this paper), I have been involved in several research projects on the dematerialization of justice systems in Canada. With colleagues across different research teams, I have studied virtual hearings and digital documents in the context of disciplinary and criminal law in Quebec. We have even written about Post-its! The central premise of this research is that the distinction between means and ends – so fundamental to jurists and other legal actors – can prevent an adequate understanding of the role both physical and digital technologies play in legal settings.

Legal technique, methodology and bureaucracies

My work adopts an “external” perspective on law but one that takes law as seriously as an “internal” perspective would. Using empirical methods, I subject legal technique to the same rigorous scrutiny that social scientists typically apply to the extra-legal factors they more often study. I reflect on this approach in a book chapter and in an article in Estudios Socio-jurídicos that reviews the scholarly literature in Anglo-Saxon socio-legal studies. Additionally, in articles published in the Journal of Law and Society and the Revista Brasileira de Direito Processual Penal, I have argued that bureaucratic institutions apply and, in practice, create criminal law and that justice systems should therefore be approached as the bureaucracies that they are.

Over the course of my career, I have worked on a range of topics and with diverse research teams. These experiences have honed my skills and broadened my general knowledge of the world. I have conducted fieldwork on such countries as Mali, Burkina Faso and Colombia, and I have been part of teams researching topics as diverse as religious radicalization, mining communities, immigration policies, TV series and climate change adaptation.
